# Inflation Expectations — Paper Summaries

Headline quotes are verbatim with page numbers as printed in the file edition present. Two files are pre-publication versions whose pagination differs from the published citation (flagged below).

---

## Cagan (1956)

- **Title:** The Monetary Dynamics of Hyperinflation (chapter in *Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money*, ed. Friedman, Univ. of Chicago Press)
- **Year:** 1956
- **Authors:** Phillip Cagan
- **Data:** Monthly money (M, hand-to-hand currency, plus bank deposits except Greece/Russia) and price indexes for seven hyperinflations — Austria (Oct 1921–Aug 1922), Germany (Aug 1922–Nov 1923), Greece (Nov 1943–Nov 1944), Hungary post-WWI (Mar 1923–Feb 1924), Hungary post-WWII (Aug 1945–Jul 1946), Poland (Jan 1923–Jan 1924), Russia (Dec 1921–Jan 1924). Sources: League of Nations memoranda, national statistical offices, secondary sources.
- **Strategy:** Posits real money demand as a function of expected inflation alone, log(M/P) = −αE − γ, with expectations adaptive (dE/dt = β(C − E)); fits the combined equation by least squares to each country, choosing β to maximize fit, recovering α and β.
- **Headline:** Real money balances during hyperinflation are governed almost entirely by adaptively-formed expected inflation; the estimated demand elasticities are too low for self-generating inflation, so the episodes were money-driven and stoppable by halting money growth.

> "Apparently the slope and the lag never reached the critical level in the seven hyperinflations, for none had self-generating price increases. Instead of running away on their own, price increases remained closely linked to past and current changes in the quantity of money and could have been stopped at any time, as they finally were, by tapering off the issue of new money." — p. 88 (PDF p. 33)

## Muth (1961)

- **Title:** Rational Expectations and the Theory of Price Movements
- **Year:** 1961 (*Econometrica* 29(3):315–335)
- **Authors:** John F. Muth
- **Data:** None — purely theoretical. References prior empirical work (Heady-Kaldor, Theil, commodity cycles) only as motivation.
- **Strategy:** Posits that subjective expectations equal the objective conditional expectation implied by the relevant model; solves a linear supply-demand market with a production lag (and inventory speculation) and compares time-series properties against cobweb expectations.
- **Headline:** If expectations are formed rationally — equal to the model's predictions — the expected price is an unbiased predictor of the actual price, reconciling theory with observed expectations data that the cobweb model cannot.

> "In order to explain these phenomena, I should like to suggest that expectations, since they are informed predictions of future events, are essentially the same as the predictions of the relevant economic theory." — p. 316 (PDF p. 3)

(Formal statement: "The rational expectations hypothesis states that, in the aggregate, the expected price is an unbiased predictor of the actual price." — p. 333, PDF p. 20)

## Friedman (1968)

- **Title:** The Role of Monetary Policy
- **Year:** 1968 (*American Economic Review* 58(1); AEA presidential address, 29 Dec 1967)
- **Authors:** Milton Friedman
- **Data:** None formal — conceptual address. Draws illustratively on U.S. monetary history (1929–33 contraction, postwar Fed episodes) and South American inflation.
- **Strategy:** No econometrics. Theoretical reasoning built on the nominal/real distinction (natural rate of interest, natural rate of unemployment) and inflation expectations (Fisher effect) to show monetary policy can peg nominal but not real magnitudes except temporarily.
- **Headline:** There is no permanent inflation–unemployment trade-off; monetary policy can hold unemployment below the natural rate only temporarily via accelerating unanticipated inflation, so its proper role is steady, moderate money growth.

> "To state this conclusion differently, there is always a temporary trade-off between inflation and unemployment; there is no permanent trade-off. The temporary trade-off comes not from inflation per se, but from unanticipated inflation, which generally means, from a rising rate of inflation." — p. 11 (PDF p. 11)

## Armantier, Topa, van der Klaauw & Zafar (2016/2017)

- **Title:** An Overview of the Survey of Consumer Expectations
- **Year:** Document dated November 2016 (NY Fed Staff Report no. 800); **filename says 2017** (the published *Economic Policy Review* version).
- **Authors:** Olivier Armantier, Giorgio Topa, Wilbert van der Klaauw, Basit Zafar
- **Data:** The NY Fed Survey of Consumer Expectations (SCE) — nationally representative monthly internet panel of ~1,300 household heads (started June 2013), covering inflation, household finance, labor, housing; comparison stats over Oct 2013–Sep 2015, weighted to the ACS.
- **Strategy:** Descriptive measurement/methodology, not identification. Documents probabilistic elicitation (Manski), sampling and raking to ACS, and statistic construction — interpolated quantiles and fitted subjective densities (generalized Beta) per Engelberg-Manski-Williams.
- **Headline:** Introduces and documents the SCE, showing consumers understand inflation and can express it numerically and that probabilistic/density formats yield comparable, informative measures of beliefs and uncertainty.

> "Of the 5,179 respondents, 82% reported 5 or lower, with just 5.6% choosing 7 on the scale. Overall, this is pretty convincing evidence that the vast majority of consumers understands the concept of inflation and is able to express it numerically." — p. 7 (PDF p. 9)

## Coibion & Gorodnichenko (2010 WP / 2015)

- **Title:** Information Rigidity and the Expectations Formation Process: A Simple Framework and New Facts
- **Year:** File is the **NBER WP 16537, November 2010** version; **filename says 2015** (published *AER*).
- **Authors:** Olivier Coibion, Yuriy Gorodnichenko
- **Data:** Professional-forecaster surveys: U.S. SPF (inflation 1969–2010; other macro variables from 1968Q4/1981), Livingston Survey, Michigan Survey, Cleveland Fed market-based expectations, and Consensus Economics for 12 countries (G-7 from 1989).
- **Strategy:** Tests full-information rational expectations (FIRE) by regressing the mean forecast error on the mean forecast revision; β > 0 rejects FIRE and maps one-to-one into the degree of information rigidity under both sticky-information and imperfect-information models.
- **Headline:** Predictable positive coefficients on forecast revisions, across agents/variables/countries, reject FIRE in favor of information-rigidity models, implying economically large frictions (information updated roughly every six to seven months).

> "From 1969-2010, we find β̂ = 1.23 (0.50) as shown in Panel B of Table 1. As a result, we can reject the null of FIRE at the 5% level of statistical significance in a manner that is directly informative about the expectations formation process." — p. 10 (PDF p. 12)

## Candia, Coibion & Gorodnichenko (2021)

- **Title:** The Inflation Expectations of U.S. Firms: Evidence from a New Survey
- **Year:** 2021 (NBER WP 28836, May 2021)
- **Authors:** Bernardo Candia, Olivier Coibion, Yuriy Gorodnichenko
- **Data:** The new Survey of Firms' Inflation Expectations (SoFIE), quarterly, 13 waves April 2018–April 2021, 300–600 firms/wave, 1,198 firms total; benchmarked against Michigan Survey, SPF, FOMC projections, Cleveland Fed, Atlanta Fed BIE, Nielsen Homescan.
- **Strategy:** Descriptive. Constructs time-series and cross-sectional moments of firms' expectations, benchmarks against the five anchoring criteria of Kumar et al. (2015), and runs OLS/Huber regressions of expectations on perceived recent inflation and perceived Fed target. No causal identification.
- **Headline:** U.S. firms' inflation expectations are far from anchored — high, dispersed, uncertain, heavily revised, like households' and unlike professionals' — driven by managerial inattention to recent inflation and the Fed's target.

> "By any typical definition of "anchored" expectations, the inflation expectations of U.S. managers appear far from anchored, much like those of households. And like households, U.S. managers are largely uninformed about recent aggregate inflation dynamics or monetary policy." — Abstract, unnumbered (PDF p. 2)
